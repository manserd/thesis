\section{\exprepl}

In \exprepl, we aim to establish that our implementation is correct and that it performs similarly enough to the implementation used by \tcite{tomasz2024} for results to be comparable across both works. To do so, we replicate the first experiment performed by \tcite{tomasz2024}, which compares coverage numbers between GBFS, \hgUu (which they refer to as Type-GBFS), and all variants of the new algorithms.

Coverage is measured on a suite of problems from IPC 2011 and 2014. We call this suite the \kw{reference suite}, and it includes the following domains:

{\small
\begin{tabular}{ccc}
    \texttt{barman-sat14-strips}&\texttt{childsnack-sat14-strips}&\texttt{elevators-sat11-strips}

    \\&\texttt{woodworking-sat11-strips}

There are a few caveats to this experiment: first, \tcite{tomasz2024} reported the total number of problems for their small experiment at 376, while our reference suite covers 380. Assume therefore that our numbers are slightly higher than theirs by default.

Second, coverage can be affected by hardware differences and noise: we tried to keep both these factors as small as we could, but some difference will remain. And third, all stochastic algorithms inherently behave differently depending on the configuration of the random number generator, which we were unable to match between their experiment and ours: we try to minimize the uncertainty by averaging our results across multiple runs, but here too, some difference will remain.

While \Cref{tbl:repldomains1} and \Cref{tbl:repldomains2} show per-domain results of this experiment, \Cref{tbl:replsum} is the most insightful: for most configurations, the coverages are close enough for us to consider it reasonable to attribute the differences to the aforementioned caveats, but three outliers are worth pointing out: \hiHu, \hiHh, and \lwHh show a noticeably higher coverage in our experiment compared to theirs. Since we do not have access to \tcite{tomasz2024}'s per-domain numbers for these configurations, we unfortunately cannot determine if this difference is isolated to specific domains.

We still deem this experiment a success, especially since discrepancies between the numbers appear primarily for ``mediocre''-performing configurations. This is shown by the numbers in parentheses to the right of every coverage number: these give the ``ranking'' of each result for those algorithms where the coverage is available for both works. The rankings roughly agree regarding the best and worst entries, indicating that the implementations agree regarding which configurations perform the best and which perform the worst.

\section{\expbt}

Recall that $\Softmin$ has a temperature parameter $\tau>0$. This parameter allows us to control how strongly biased selection strategies prefer towards lower values: at low $\tau$, the strategy almost always selects the lowest or one of the lowest values, whereas for larger $\tau$, biased selection behaves more and more like uniform selection. Since we can control $\tau$ independently for type selection as well as state selection, going forward, we will use $\tau_\text{types}$ when discussing the former, and $\tau_\text{states}$ for the latter.

In \expbt, we test those algorithms that use biased type selection with different $\tau_\text{types}$ to see if some variants might benefit from more or less aggressive type biasing. Due to the larger number of runs, we separate this experiment into two sub-experiments.

\subsection{\expbtone}

In \expbtone, we only use a subset of the reference suite. We call this the \kw{reduced suite}, and it is identical to the reference suite, except that it removes some domains where \exprepl\ (\Cref{tbl:repldomains1} and \Cref{tbl:repldomains2}) has shown all algorithms consistently reaching full coverage or no coverage at all. In particular, we omit \texttt{childsnack-sat14-strips}, where all algorithms consistenly reached zero coverage, and we omit all the following domains where all algorithms consistently reached full coverage:

{\small
\begin{tabular}{ccc}
    \texttt{ged-sat14-strips}&\texttt{hiking-sat14-strips}&\texttt{parcprinter-sat11-strips}
    \\\texttt{pegsol-sat11-strips}&\texttt{scanalyzer-sat11-strips}&\texttt{sokoban-sat11-strips}
\end{tabular}
}

When comparing coverage numbers from this experiment to other experiments, we assume that all of these domains would have shown the same coverage here as they did with $\tau_\text{types}=1$ in \exprepl.
 
We also only test a subset of the algorithms: namely, we exclude all variants that use the \Utss type selection strategy, since this strategy does not use $\Softmin$, and we only include variants that use the \Usss state selection strategy. The latter assumes that the type and state selection strategies affect an algorithm's performance independently of each other: we have no reason to assume this to be true and thus acknowledge this as a potential gap in our methodology.

\subsection{\expbttwo}

The goal of \expbttwo\ is to evaluate the most promising algorithms of \expbtone\ on the full reference suite and with temperatures that reach further into the directions that appeared promising.

In \expbtone, \lwHu and \lwDu achieved higher coverage at reduced temperatures: therefore, in this experiment, we additionally test these variants at even lower temperatures. Also, because this experiment omits the HI variants and tests fewer different temperatures overall, we are now also able to evaluate the \Hsss variants of LW.

As \hgHu only showed an improvement at $\tau_\text{types}=2$ and not beyond, and since there are no other state selection strategies for this algorithm, our only goal for this variant is to see if its coverage on the full reference suite reflects the predicted reference suite coverage from the first stage.

First, observe in \Cref{tbl:bttwodomains1} and \Cref{tbl:bttwodomains2} that the domains we omitted in \expbtone\ show the same coverage here as in \exprepl, with only \texttt{ged} showing slightly lower numbers. We can therefore reasonably assume that those variants that we tested on the reduced suite in \expbtone\ would likely produce coverage numbers similar to those we predicted in \Cref{tbl:btonesum} when evaluated on the full reference suite.

Second, \Cref{tbl:bttwosum} shows that \lwHh consistently managed to solve about 5-10 more problems than \lwHu, which matches the relative improvement we observed between these variants in \exprepl. The same applies for \lwDu and \lwDh, albeit with a smaller difference. We therefore assume that, at least for the configurations we tested, changing $\tau_\text{types}$ did not significantly alter the effectiveness of the state selection strategy.

Third, we observe no additional improvements compared to \expbtone\ at more extreme values of $\tau_\text{types}$. This matches our expectations, since $\tau_\text{types}=0.125$ will already select the lowest value most of the time.

Additionally, notice that the coverage numbers of identical configurations that we tested both in \expbtone\ and \expbttwo\ frequently show a difference of up to five problems between both experiments. This indicates that differences in coverage between values of $\tau_\text{types}$ are not definitive since they could be caused purely by noise.

We conclude this section with some closing remarks regarding both \expbt\ and \expst. In both experiments, raising the $\Softmin$ temperature showed no consistent improvement. This aligns with our expectations, since raising the temperature pushes biased strategies toward behaving like uniform strategies, and uniform strategies most often perform similar to or worse than biased ones, as we observed in \exprepl. Lowering the temperature produced some minor improvements in some configurations, but none that were severe enough to definitively show that the improvements were not simply the product of noise. We therefore think it is reasonable to assume that the default temperature $\tau=1$ for both type and state selection is sufficiently close to optimal for all tested configurations.
