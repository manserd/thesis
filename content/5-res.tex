\chapter{Evaluation}

In this chapter we outline the experiments we ran to evaluate the HI and LW type systems and we discuss our observations. As benchmarks we used satisficing problems from the \kw{International Planning Competition}[IPC], which we retrieved from the Fast Downward benchmarks repository\footnote{\url{https://github.com/aibasel/downward-benchmarks}}. Specifically, we used the same IPC 2011 and 2014 domains that \tcite{tomasz2024} used in their first experiment, except that our suite totalled 380 problems whereas \tcite{tomasz2024} report their total number of problems at 376. We were unable to determine where this difference comes from: assume therefore that when comparing coverage numbers between both works, our numbers are slightly higher by default.

All experiments were run on an AMD Epyc 7742 CPU, with a 3584MiB memory limit and 10 minute time limit per task. Aside from the CPU, this matches \tcite{tomasz2024}'s experimental setup, which was done to keep the coverage numbers somewhat comparable across both works. For this same reason, we also used the \kw{FF} heuristic \pcite{hoffmann2001}, treated all problems as unit-cost problems, and used the same default $\Softmin$ temperature of $\tau=1$ for algorithms that perform biased type or state selection, unless specified otherwise. Finally, for stochastic algorithms, all results represent averages over five runs.

\section{Comparison to Original Work}

We first aimed to establish that our implementation is correct and that it performs similarly to the implementation used by \tcite{tomasz2024}. To do so, we replicated the first experiment performed by \tcite{tomasz2024}, which compares coverage numbers between GBFS, \hgUu (which they refer to as Type-GBFS), and all variants of the new algorithms.

We point out two additional caveats that apply to this comparison in particular, but will also remain relevant for all other results. First, coverage can be affected by hardware differences and noise: this is particularly relevant here, where we are comparing results obtained on different systems. And second, all stochastic algorithms inherently behave differently depending on the configuration of the random number generator, which we were unable to match between the experiments performed by \tcite{tomasz2024} and ours: in both works, the results are averaged across multiple runs, but some difference will remain.

\Cref{tbl:replsum} shows a comparison between the coverage numbers reported by \tcite{tomasz2024} and those obtained by us. Our implementation agrees with \tcite{tomasz2024}'s findings that the configurations that pair the heuristic improvement type system with depth-biased type selection, that is \hiDh and \hiDu, perform by far the best.

Both works then generally agree again regarding which configurations perform the worst, those being \lwUu, \hiUu, \hgUu, and \gbfs, all of which only perform unbiased type and state selection, or no stochastic exploration at all.

Finally, we want to point to GBFS: since this is a deterministic algorithm, and since both works likely used the exact same implementation for it, we assume that the delta in coverage which we record here is mostly attributable to hardware differences. Assuming, therefore, that our coverage numbers are generally about 5-7 higher than theirs by default, it is notable that we nevertheless measure equal or even worse coverage for some configurations: namely, \hgHu and \hiUu show around the same coverage across both works, and \hgUu is slightly worse in our case.

Both with these outliers, as well as with those configurations that scored higher than expected, it would be interesting to investigate if this primarily occurs in specific domains. Unfortunately, since we do not have access to \tcite{tomasz2024}'s per-domain coverage numbers for all configurations, we cannot do this at this time.

To measure differences in the lengths of the solutions produced by different algorithms, we isolate all those problems that all configurations were able to solve within the time and memory constraints, and sum their individual solution lengths. The resulting values are shown in \Cref{tbl:replpath}: all configurations produce similar solution lengths, with the exception of those that use the heuristic improvement type system paired with depth-biased type selection, which produce much longer solutions. Note that the variants that use the low-water mark type system do not show this same behavior.

Consider that LW tends to group longer sequences of states into the same type than HI: for example, along a path $\langle s_1,s_2,s_3,s_4,s_5\rangle$, and given some heuristic $h$ such that $h(s_1)=h(s_3)=h(s_5)=1$ and $h(s_2)=h(s_4)=2$, HI will identify both $s_2$ and $s_4$ as progress states, leading to the creation of new types, while LW will consider all these states part of the same type. The consequence of this is that the type tree (\Cref{sec:typetrees}) induced by HI naturally grows deeper than the one induced by LW, which allows HI to have better depth-based diversification than LW. As \tcite{asai2017} have shown that a lack of depth-based diversification can lead to worse performance, this offers one possible explanation for why LW tends to perform worse than HI.

\section{Softmin Temperature}

Recall that $\Softmin$ has a temperature parameter $\tau>0$. This parameter allows us to control how strongly biased selection strategies prefer lower values: at low $\tau$, the strategy almost always selects the lowest or one of the lowest values, whereas for larger $\tau$, biased selection behaves more and more like uniform selection. Since we can control $\tau$ independently for type selection as well as state selection, going forward, we will use $\tau_\text{types}$ when discussing the former, and $\tau_\text{states}$ for the latter.

In this experiment, we test those algorithms that use biased strategies with different $\Softmin$ temperatures to see if some variants might benefit from more or less aggressive biasing. Due to the large number of runs, we test $\tau_\text{types}$ and $\tau_\text{states}$ separately.

\subsection{Type Selection Temperature}

\Cref{tbl:btsum} shows the coverage numbers under different $\tau_\text{types}$ for all algorithms that use biased type selection. Note that the results for $\tau_\text{types}=1$ were taken from the first experiment. We start with the observation that decreased type selection temperatures seem primarily harmful, since almost all configurations achieve worse coverage at and below $\tau_\text{types}=0.5$ compared to $\tau_\text{types}=1$. The two exceptions to this are \hiDh and \lwDu, notably both configurations that use depth biasing, which showed a slight improvement at reduced temperatures.

However, both these algorithms, as well \hgHu, \hiHu, \hiDh, \lwHu, and \lwDh performed best at slightly increased temperatures, suggesting that the default biasing temperature is slightly too aggressive for the majority of configurations. As temperatures rise even further, almost all algorithms perform considerably worse than at the default temperature: this matches our expectations, since biased selection behaves more and more like uniform selection as temperatures increase, and configurations that use uniform type selection generally perform worse than those that use biased selection (see \Cref{tbl:replsum}).

Next, observe the delta in coverage between configurations that only differ in their state selection strategy, as shown in \Cref{fig:bttrend}: all variants with uniform state selection except for \hiDu consistently perform worse than their variants with biased state selection as long as the type selection temperature is not too high. Moreover, in the cases of \hiHh vs. \hiHu and \lwHh vs. \lwHu, we additionally observe the variants gradually approaching each other in terms of coverage: this suggests that in these cases, changing the type selection temperature has no noticeable effect on the performance of the state selection strategy. The obvious outliers are \lwDh vs. \lwDu and \hiDh vs. \hiDu: in the case of the former two configurations, we see a sudden jump in the performance of \lwDu at $\tau_\text{types}=2$ where it almost matches the performance of \lwDh, whereas the latter two are the only pair of variants where random state selection consistently outperforms biased state selection.

Lastly, we point to \hgHu: this algorithm showed by far the strongest relative improvement, solving around ten additional problems at $\tau_\text{types}=2$ compared to $\tau_\text{types}=1$. This is particularly relevant since this improvement places \hgHu ahead of all those configurations that use the low-water mark improvement type system. \tcite{tomasz2024} observed this same phenomenom at the default softmin temperature, and our data suggests that it remains accurate at increased temperatures, too.

As for solution lengths, \Cref{tbl:btpath} suggests that the lengths generally decrease as the type selection temperature increases. This is especially pronounced with \hiDu and \hiDh, where the sum of the solution lengths decreases by around 40\% as the temperature rises from $\tau_\text{types}=0.25$ to $\tau_\text{types}=10$; \hiDu and \hiDh are generally our best-performing configurations coverage-wise, but they usually suffer from much higher solution lengths compared to the other algorithms. This observation suggests that for a certain class of problems, it might be possible to mitigate this by raising the type selection temperature. This could be particularly effective with \hiDh, whose coverage seems to drop less at higher temperatures compared to that of \hiDu (\Cref{tbl:btsum}).

\subsection{State Selection Temperature}

We next tested different state selection temperatures $\tau_\text{states}$, the results of which are shown in \Cref{tbl:stsum}. The results for $\tau_\text{states}=1$ were again taken from the first experiment. Similar to what we observed when lowering the type selection temperature, it seems that lowering the state selection temperature is also not beneficial for coverage, with no single coverage number at decreased temperature matching or surpassing the corresponding coverage at the default temperature.

We see minor improvements at elevated temperatures in the case of \hiHh and \lwDh, with larger differences for \hiUh, \hiDh, \lwHh, and, in particular, \lwUh. Notably, it is clearly visible here that biased state selection performs more and more like random state selection as the temperature rises: every single variant tends towards its unbiased counterpart as $\tau_\text{states}$ increases. Nevertheless, it seems like there is some improvement to be made regarding the state selection temperature: we record \hiUh solving almost five additional problems at $\tau_\text{states}=5$ compared to the default temperature and compared to \hiUu, and a similar improvement was measured for \lwHh at $\tau_\text{states}=2$.

Finally, recall that we generally observe biased selection strategies performing better than unbiased ones: one interesting exception to this trend, as previously observed at the default $\Softmin$ temperature, is \hiDu outperforming \hiDh, which is particularly interesting since \hiDu is the best overall configuration as found by \tcite{tomasz2024}. Our measurements show \hiDh getting close to the performance of \hiDu when its state selection temperature is raised to $\tau_\text{states}=2$: this shows that it might be possible for \hiDh to exceed the performance of \hiDu if we assume that \hiDu's lead might be isolated to the specific set of benchmarks it was thus far evaluated on.

Interestingly, our prior observation that solution lengths decrease with increased type selection temperatures does not translate to $\tau_\text{states}$: in fact, \Cref{tbl:stpath} suggests that the opposite is true for the state selection temperature. As for \hiDh, while the effect seems less pronounced here, we still observe noticeably shorter solution lengths at the low end of the temperature spectrum. However, these improvements are less attractive than those we observed with the type selection temperature, since low $\tau_\text{states}$ leads to a considerable drop in \hiDh's coverage (\Cref{tbl:btsum}).

We conclude this experiment with some closing remarks. Since we were unable to test simultaneous modifications of both type as well as state selection temperature, we cannot conclude that the improvements we observed when adjusting either temperature would be preserved when simultaneously adjusting the other one. This is particularly relevant since some of our data seems to suggest that the type selection strategy and the state selection strategy do not only affect performance independent of each other (recall \hiDu beating \hiDh, while \hiHh beats \hiHu).

\section{Exploration-Exploitation Balance}

So far, all of our experiments, as well as those performed by \tcite{tomasz2024}, used algorithms that introduce type-based exploration by alternating between exploitation and exploration on every expansion. These algorithms therefore spend roughly half of their effort on exploitation and half on exploration. This is not necessarily the optimal balance: in their introduction of $\epsilon$-GBFS, which allows us to tune this balance via the $\epsilon$ parameter, \tcite{valenzano2014} found that it can be beneficial in some cases to allocate less time to exploration and more to exploitation.

In our next experiment, we aim to test our new type systems under different values of $\epsilon$ to see if this applies here, too. For this, we replace the alternating open list that manages exploration and exploitation in our configurations with an open list that only performs an exploratory expansion with some probability $\epsilon$. Note that when $\epsilon=0.5$, this open list approximately performs like the alternating open list if the number of expansions is sufficiently large. The range of values for $\epsilon$ that we test include values below $\epsilon=0.5$, since these were the most effective in \tcite{valenzano2014}'s work, as well as one value above $\epsilon=0.5$ to test if our algorithms could benefit from more exploration.

\Cref{tbl:epssum} shows the coverage numbers we obtained in this experiment, where the \textit{ALT} column repeats the coverages we obtained in our first experiment. Notice that the coverages we obtain when $\epsilon=0.5$ are indeed very close to those we achieved when using the alternating open list, as expected.

Our measurements identify three algorithms that may potentially benefit from more exploration, those being \hgHu, \lwHh, and \lwDh. However, the difference in coverage that we record here is small enough that it could also simply be the product of noise, so we do not consider these results conclusive.

When it comes to performing less exploration, we also do not consider the small improvement we record for \hgUu conclusive. We do, however, record considerable improvements at lower $\epsilon$ for all those configurations that use uniform type selection, namely \hiUh, \lwUu, \hiUu, and \lwUh: the former two configurations managed to solve around 6-9 additional problems at $\epsilon=0.25$ compared to $\epsilon=0.5$, while the latter two showed an even greater improvement, with around 14 additional problems solved at $\epsilon=0.25$ for \lwUh\ and at $\epsilon=0.1$ for \hiUu.

We will note that all of these improvements were achieved by algorithms that are among the worst performers at $\epsilon=0.5$, and even with these improvements, this assessment does not change. Still, it is interesting to observe configurations like \hiUu achieving such comparatively high coverage numbers: recall that a low $\epsilon$ value effectively causes these algorithms to behave more like regular GBFS. Looking at it from this perspective, we see that adding even a tiny amount of exploration to GBFS can noticeably improve the performance of the algorithm.

Now, perhaps most importantly, none of our best-performing configurations showed any improvement at different values of $\epsilon$, suggesting that an equal balance of exploration and exploitation is likely close to ideal, at least under the circumstances which we tested.

\Cref{tbl:epspath} shows that solution lengths seem largely unaffected by different $\epsilon$ values, only suggesting a slight downward trend as $\epsilon$ decreases. The only exceptions to this are, again, \hiDu and \hiDh. The results suggest that at $\epsilon=0.25$, it is possible to achieve significantly shorter solution lengths, at almost no cost in coverage.
